Why does it matter?

Why care about time? As long as AI can get the job done, why do we even care about how long it took them? Well, it's usually not the case in real-world applications.
Customer Service
Autonomous Driving
For example, customer service and autonomous driving. You don't want your user to wait forever on the phone. Or your car to think for a minute on whether to step on the brake or not in an emergency. So, in real-world applications, speed is critical.

Why now?

Then, the question is why now? If processing speed is so important, why didn't we care about it earlier? Why do we suddenly start to care about it now? To answer this question, we need to have a quick review of how we got here in the past few years.

What did we learn?

So, what did we learn from this brief history?

Buy Tokens!

First, buy tokens! Do not do anything by yourself. Just buy tokens. This is exactly what Google and OpenAI wants you to think.

More Capable

Second, models became more capable. Before deep learning, AI were not as capable. If it cannot even do the tasks, why do we care about its speed. When it became more capable, people start to use it to build applications. Then, people start to care about its speed. However, we may still not need a unified speed metric because the models and tasks were so different from one another. Even we needed speed, we were just measuring them case by case.

More General

Then, models also became more general. We have converged on the transformer architecture. And ChatGPT passed the turing test. We use the same model for everything, all the way from recommender systems to reading medical images. So, Now, it might be a good time to design a unified speed metric to measure the speed of AI.

A Mental Shift

Most importantly, we saw a mental shift.

A Mental Shift

Model

->

Service

A shift from using models to using services. By model, I mean the neural architectures and their parameters. Something that purely static and can be written on a piece of paper. By service, I mean running the models on GPUs to produce tokens and put it behind the APIs. We used to interact with models directly, we pretrain them, fine-tune them, and serve them. but now, we only use a well-encapsulated service to get tokens, we no longer know the hardware, framework, and the models behind it. For example, GPT5 dynamically routes to different models based on the query. We don't even know what models is used. Now, the user pattern shifted from using models to using services, the evaluation should shift as well. To properly evaluate the services, speed is an integral part of a service.

Why now?

Let's come back to the question of why now. Because we only start to see this shift from models to services now. So, it is a good time now to have a unified speed metric for AI.

So, we do have some speed metrics for AI today. Let's take a look.

The most popular speed metric for AI today is tokens per second, or TPS. Basically, it's about how many tokens can AI spits out per second. But this metric is a little bit indirect. What the users really care about is tasks per second, or time per task. When they ask how many tokens do I get per second, they really want to know how fast can I get my tasks done. So how do we measure that?

High TPS == Fast ?

Intuitively, we might think that high TPS means fast task completion. But is that really true?

Test-Time Scaling

Check out test-time scaling paper, which is a quite popular one from ICLR 2024. It shows that when you increase the length of the chain of thought, just let the model think a little bit more, it becomes more intelligent.

You can also ready it in another way. Extend the thinking time, and smaller models handle complex tasks just as well. So, Smaller models with more tokens can do the same as larger models with fewer tokens.

Implications

There are a couple of important implications of this.

Implications

Benchmarks Are Invalid.

High TPS != Fast

First, benchmark datasets are invalid. They no longer measure intelligence levels without considering the number of tokens used. A larger model is obviously more intelligent than a smaller model, they still can have the same score on the benchmarks. Second, high TPS does not mean fast task completion. You know how fast it can spit out tokens, but you do not know how many tokens you need to finish a task. A smaller model use 100k tokens, but a larger model may only use 1k tokens.

Multi-Modal

Useful Information Time
It is measured as useful information output by AI per unit time.

Disambiguation

Of course, there are some ambiguities here.

Disambiguation

Time: Wall Time 

Usefulness: 3 Approximations

We use wall time, not CPU time. We are using AI to interact with the real world, so we care about elapsed time in the real world not about how the compute happend behind the APIs. Second ambiguity: measuring usefulness. This seems to be a pretty subjective measure. It is challenging to measure the exact amount of useful information in the AI output, We propose 3 simple approximations.

Method 1:

Weighted Average Over Time

$$ B = \frac{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n} w_i s_i}{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n} w_i \cdot t} $$

Method 1: Weighted average over time. This looks exactly like how we compute our GPA. B is the intelligence bandwidth. w is the weight of each benchmark, s is the score AI achieved on each benchmark, The only difference is that we divide the GPA by total time elapsed during the exams, which is the t here. Here are the pros and cons. It is the most accurate way to measure usefulness. These benchmarks are designed to let AI produce useful information. Anything that is not useful, for example, a wrong answer, or their long chain of thought, is not counted. However, it is challenging to measure. You have to spend a lot of money and have all the benchmarks available to measure them.

Method 2:

The Information Theory Approach

$$ B=\frac{I'(X)}{t} $$

$$ I'(X) = -\sum_{i=1}^{n} \log_2 P(x_i | x_1, x_2, \dots, x_{i-1}) $$

The second method is an Information theory approach. It measures total amount of information in output. B is the intelligence bandwidth. X is the output I'(X) is the total information in the output. t is the time. For example, if it is a language model, I'(X) can be computed like this using conditional probability. Given all the previous tokens, x1 to x(i-1), how likely is the next token x(i) would be. And we use the log to compute the information in bits just like how we compute the entropy. The problem of this method is it is not easy to measure. For example, to commpute P, you need to have access to the probability vector output by the language model. You can not get it from the APIs. Another problem is that it assumes all the output is useful, which is not always true.

Method 3:

Raw Output Bits

KiloBytes per Second (KB/s)

The third method is the simplest. It measures raw output in KB/s. It is super simple to measure and works across modalities. The problem is also obvious, output from AI may not always be useful. However, we can fix that by constraining it to the most high speed-quality AI services, like ChatGPT and Gemini. And trust them, their outputs are mostly useful.

Summary

1. Weighted average over time

2. The information theory approach

3. Raw output bits (KB/s)

A quick summary. Method 1 is the most accurate, but the hardest to measure. So, if you are an application builder, you may want to choose method 1 to evaluate the models you are using. However, because Method 3 is super simple to measure, it can give you a first pass across all the models to get a smaller subset for further evaluation. And a good metric does not just end there. Good metrics discovers new growth patterns. For example, feature width led to Moore's Law, The number of transistors on a chip doubles every two years. It is actually based on the density of transistors, which is directly related to the metric of feature width. Another example, compute performance led to Huang's Law. The performance is measured in FLOPS, which doubles less than every two years. Another example, network bandwidth led to Nielsen's Law. The bandwidth available to high-end users grows by approximately 50% annually. Why are these laws useful? You can actually use them to predict future trends. For example, with Nielsen's Law, you can predict the emergence of twitter, pinterest, tiktok, and YouTube. cause higher bandwidth enabled users to consume multimedia content. So, what about intelligence bandwidth? Is there a new growth pattern waiting to be discovered about AI?
By fitting a line through the top models, we see exponential growth in KB/s for top models. Excluding the speed-quality tradeoff of Gemini 2.5 Flash, the line fits between Imagen 3 and Imagen 4, which are the best image generators with highest fidelity. This is a rather conservative estimate.

It says: Peak AI output rate in KB/s doubles every year. We measure only the best public APIs to ensure useful output. We select the modality with the highest intelligence bandwidth. Now, it is image generation. One day, video might take the lead.

Human AI Interaction

Jin's Law helps predict human-AI interaction shifts. Before we get into the actual predictions. Let's quickly review a few basics in human-AI interaction.

Human AI Interaction

Self-Paced

Audio

There are two main types of human-AI interaction: Self-paced and fixed-speed. The self-paced includes text and images, where users go at their own speed. The fixed-speed includes audio and video, where the users follows the playback speed. For self-paced ones, AI's output speed needs to exceeds human reading speed to enable real-time interaction. Text, For example, we as humans can read at about 200 to 300 words per minute. while AI can generate 14,000 words per minute, which is way faster than human reading speed. So, it allows us to do real-time text-based interactions with AI. For fixed-speed ones, AI's output speed needs to match the playback speed for real-time interaction. Audio, for example, as long as it can generate 1s of audio in 1s, we can real-time audio interactions with AI. Today, the best model can generate 100 seconds of audio in 1 second. So, we can already do audio interactions with AI. For images, and videos, we are not there yet. Now, I will show you how to apply Jin's Law to predict when we can do real-time interactions with images and videos.

Limitations

Now, let's talk about the limitations of this work I presented. I did all of these independently from my day job. I have no pressure to publish it on a top conference, so I can be 100% honest about the limitations. This is also my style of doing my presentations. Fun fact: When you really do not have the pressure to get the paper accepted, it will be rejected. This paper is rejected by arXiv. Limitations

1. The approximations

2. The doubling period

3. The growth plateau

There are three main limitations of this work. First, the approximations. The three methods to approximate usefulness we have proposed are all pretty straight-forward. They can definitely be improved. We are just here to point out the problem and provide a basic solution to kickstart the discussion. Second, the doubling period of intelligence bandwidth, which is 1 year, as we stated in Jin's Law, still has some uncertainty. We made this prediciton based on a limited number of samples we can possibly collect as we are still in a primitive stage of GenAI. As we collect more data, we may have a more accurate estimation of the doubling period. Third, the growth of intelligence bandwidth may plateau someday in the future. Just like many people saying that it is the end of Moore's Law, Jin's Law would not last forever either. The number one risk to the exponential growth is the AI bubbles. We saw a lot of hundreds million packages from Meta to poach talent from other big tech companies. This is not sustainable. If the bubble bursts, the growth of AI would slow down. The second risk to the exponential growth is the energy supply. We are building data centers that consumes more energy than we can produce. You all heard of the stargate project from OpenAI. Projects like this are all over the country. And, 20% of them will be delayed to connect to the power grid due to the shortage of power supply. So, there are risks that the exponential growth may not be sustainable.
